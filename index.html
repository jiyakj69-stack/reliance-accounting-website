01 — Company Research

Reliance Industries Limited

India's largest diversified business group, run not as one company but as a network of subsidiaries, intermediate holding companies, associates and joint ventures.

Reliance's traditional strength in energy and petrochemicals has broadened into five major engines: Oil‑to‑Chemicals, Oil & Gas, Digital Services, Retail, and Media & Entertainment. The digital business is organised under Jio Platforms Limited, and the retail business under Reliance Retail Ventures Limited (RRVL) — each acting as an intermediate holding layer in its own right, rather than RIL owning every operating company directly.

For this project, RIL is treated as the parent / ultimate holding company of the group. The annual report separately identifies subsidiaries, associates and joint ventures, and control can run directly from RIL or through an intermediate holding company such as RRVL or Jio Platforms — which is exactly the layered structure this project uses to explain multi‑tier consolidation.

02 — Accounting Concepts

Holding & subsidiary companies, under Ind AS 110

The accounting foundation the rest of the project rests on: what makes one company a "holding company" over another, and when their statements must be combined.

Control has three parts

Ind AS 110 does not ask "how many shares?" first — it asks whether all three of these exist together:

PowerThe ability to direct the investee's relevant activities — the decisions that most affect its returns.
Exposure to variable returnsReturns from the investee that can rise or fall based on its performance — dividends, fees, residual value.
Ability to use power to affect returnsThe link between the two: using that power actually changes the size of the returns received.
Control = Power + Exposure to Variable Returns + Ability to Use Power to Affect Returns

A holding (parent) company is a company that controls one or more other companies. The companies it controls are its subsidiaries. Control may arise through voting rights, contractual arrangements, or other rights that let the parent direct the subsidiary's relevant activities — not only through owning more than 50% of the shares.

A company is not automatically a subsidiary just because another company has invested in it. The test under Ind AS 110 is control, not the size of the shareholding alone.

Ownership is the percentage of shares or voting rights held. Control is the ability to direct an investee's relevant activities and to influence the returns it receives. In practice the two usually move together, but a company can control another with less than 50% ownership (e.g. through board appointment rights), or hold a large stake without control (e.g. a passive financial investor).

Ind AS 110 sets out the principles for preparing consolidated financial statements whenever an entity controls one or more other entities. Its core idea: a parent and its subsidiaries are presented as one single economic entity, even though each remains a separate legal company.

  • Combine the assets, liabilities, income and expenses of the parent and subsidiary, line by line.
  • Eliminate intra‑group balances and transactions (e.g. intercompany receivables/payables, unrealised profit on intra‑group sales).
  • Identify and present the Non‑Controlling Interest (NCI), where the parent owns less than 100%.
  • Apply consistent accounting policies across the group for similar transactions.

NCI is the portion of a subsidiary's equity that is not attributable to the parent. If a parent owns 80% of a subsidiary, the remaining 20% held by other shareholders is the NCI — shown separately within consolidated equity, not netted off.

RIL's own consolidated accounts illustrate this directly: RIL owns 83.56% of Reliance Retail Ventures Limited, so the remaining 16.44% held by outside investors becomes NCI on consolidation — used throughout Section 05 and the simulator in Section 06.

04 — RIL × 7‑India Convenience Retail

A real second‑tier subsidiary, with real financial statements

7‑India Convenience Retail Limited is the company that actually runs 7‑Eleven stores in India. It is a wholly owned subsidiary of Reliance Retail Ventures Limited — which is itself 83.56%‑owned by RIL. That makes it a textbook case of a step‑down (second‑tier) subsidiary inside a multi‑level group.

How the relationship works

In October 2021, RRVL — through its wholly owned subsidiary 7‑India Convenience Retail Limited — signed a master franchise agreement with 7‑Eleven, Inc. to open and operate 7‑Eleven convenience stores across India, starting in Mumbai. RRVL is, in turn, a subsidiary of RIL and the holding company for all of Reliance's retail businesses.

So RIL does not hold shares in 7‑India directly. Its relationship runs RIL → RRVL → 7‑India: control flows down the chain, and on consolidation, 7‑India's assets, liabilities, income and expenses are combined into the group accounts because RIL ultimately controls it, even without a direct shareholding.

Why this matters for consolidation

Because 7‑India is 100% owned by RRVL, no Non‑Controlling Interest arises at the 7‑India level itself. But RRVL is only 83.56% owned by RIL — so when 7‑India's results are rolled all the way up into RIL's consolidated accounts, an effective (indirect) NCI of 16.44% still applies, simply because it passes through RRVL. This is the "effective ownership" calculation below.

Findings

What the study shows

Control, not ownership %, is the test

RIL's group includes intermediate holding companies (Jio Platforms, RRVL) with outside investors — consolidation still applies at the group level because control, not a simple 50% threshold, determines a subsidiary.

Multi‑tier structures need effective ownership

7‑India is 100% owned by RRVL, yet RIL's effective economic interest in it is only 83.56% once RRVL's own 16.44% outside stake is factored in — a direct, worked example of indirect ownership.

Standalone and consolidated tell different stories

RIL's standalone FY 2025‑26 profit (₹43,851 cr) is not the same figure as its consolidated FY 2024‑25 profit attributable to owners (₹81,309 cr / ₹69,648 cr per Goutham's workings) — the gap is exactly the results of subsidiaries like RRVL, Jio and 7‑India.

NCI is material to the RIL group

₹1,66,426 cr of RIL's consolidated equity, and ₹11,661 cr of its FY 2024‑25 profit, belong to non‑controlling shareholders — not a rounding error, but a structurally important slice of the group accounts.

A subsidiary can be loss‑making within a profitable group

7‑India recorded a loss of ₹89.89 cr in FY 2025‑26 (up from ₹72.94 cr in FY 2024‑25) even as RIL as a whole remained solidly profitable — consolidation absorbs both, line by line.

Debt, not equity, is funding 7‑India's growth

7‑India's cumulative OFCDs reached ₹38,475 lakh by 31 Mar 2026 against paid‑up equity capital of just ₹4,500 lakh — a financing pattern that will matter when RRVL's own consolidated accounts are analysed.

Conclusion

Reliance Industries Limited is a useful case for corporate accounting precisely because it is not a simple parent‑and‑one‑subsidiary structure. Control cascades through intermediate holding companies — most visibly Reliance Retail Ventures Limited — down to operating businesses such as 7‑India Convenience Retail Limited, the entity that actually runs 7‑Eleven stores in India. Applying Ind AS 110's control test, calculating Non‑Controlling Interest at both the RRVL level (16.44%) and effectively at the 7‑India level (also 16.44%, passed through), and comparing RIL's standalone accounts against its consolidated ones together show why "control → subsidiary → consolidation" is the operating principle of group accounting, not share ownership alone.
